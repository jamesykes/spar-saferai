\begin{abstract}
SaferAI-style quantitative risk models require many uncertain input distributions. When elicitation budget is limited, a natural question is whether additional elicitation effort should be allocated uniformly or preferentially to inputs for which the current model output is most fragile. This draft reports a scoped retrospective budget-recovery experiment for the OC3 Denial-of-Service risk model, restricted to the SOTA capability condition and the \(\psuccess\) submodel. The budget unit is one additional LLM elicitation draw. The experiment compares a step-balanced uniform allocation baseline with several leave-one-out (LOO) output-fragility-guided allocation heuristics. The goal is not to derive an optimal acquisition function, a Bayesian experimental design procedure, or a value-of-information estimate. It is to test whether a simple output-fragility heuristic contains enough signal to recover a full-data reference distribution faster than uniform allocation in this dataset.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

SaferAI-style quantitative AI risk models often require eliciting many uncertain input distributions. If elicitation effort is expensive, repeated, or rate-limited, then the allocation of additional elicitation draws becomes a practical modelling question. A uniform strategy is simple and defensible: each uncertain input receives additional draws at roughly the same rate. However, a model's output uncertainty may be more sensitive to some inputs than to others. This motivates a retrospective diagnostic question: can we use output fragility in the current partially revealed model to decide where to allocate additional elicitation draws?

This project evaluates that question in a deliberately scoped setting. It asks whether a simple output-fragility heuristic can improve recovery of a full-data reference distribution under a limited elicitation budget. The central experiment compares LOO-fragility-guided allocation of additional LLM elicitation draws against a step-balanced uniform allocation baseline. The recovery target is the full-data exchangeable nodewise reference distribution for the OC3 Denial-of-Service \(\psuccess\) submodel.

The claim is modest. The method is an active-learning-inspired heuristic, but it is not optimal active learning. It is not Bayesian experimental design, and it is not a true value-of-information calculation. LOO fragility measures finite-sample instability of a current nodewise mixture; it does not prove that an additional draw at that input will reduce error. The experiment is therefore best read as a pilot diagnostic: does this kind of output-fragility signal appear useful enough to warrant further investigation?

\section{Background and motivation}

The broader motivation is elicitation prioritisation in structured quantitative risk models. In a multi-input risk model, collecting additional probability distributions for every input may be infeasible. A model builder may want an allocation rule that spends more effort on inputs where current uncertainty is downstream-relevant. The natural baseline is uniform allocation across model inputs. A useful heuristic should be compared against this baseline on a fixed retrospective dataset, where all candidate elicitation draws are already available but are hidden from the policy until revealed.

The present experiment follows the SaferAI cyber-risk modelling context at a limited proof-of-concept scale \citep{saferai-methodology-placeholder, saferai-cyber-risk-placeholder}. It uses MITRE ATT\&CK technique labels to define the leaf inputs in the OC3 Denial-of-Service success-probability submodel \citep{mitre-attack}. The evaluation metric is squared Wasserstein-2 distance between one-dimensional empirical output distributions, equivalent in this setting to integrated squared quantile discrepancy \citep{villani2009optimal}.

\section{Experiment scope}

The final-report scope is intentionally narrow:

\begin{tabular}{ll}

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The experiment does not model the full total-risk equation. It excludes multiplicative factors such as the number of actors, attempts per actor, and damage conditional on success. The primary output is
\[
T(M)=\law\left(P_M(\mathrm{success})\right),
\]
where \(M\) is the current collection of nodewise elicitation mixtures and \(\law(X)\) denotes the law, or probability distribution, of \(X\). It does not denote a likelihood.

The analysis is retrospective. The full-data reference distribution is not ground truth. It is the output distribution obtained by using all valid SOTA LLM elicitation rows under the exchangeable nodewise mixture approximation.

\section{Data and preprocessing}

The raw dataset contains 3600 rows:
\[
40 \text{ repeats} \times 5 \text{ LLM forecaster models} \times 9 \text{ MITRE-step inputs} \times 2 \text{ capability levels}.
\]
Rows include elicited probability quartiles \(\texttt{percentile\_25th}\), \(\texttt{percentile\_50th}\), and \(\texttt{percentile\_75th}\). The \(\texttt{estimate}\) column is treated as equivalent to the median only for sanity checks; fitting uses the quartile columns directly.

The cleaning layer excludes rows with API or parsing errors, missing quartiles, invalid quartile ordering, or quartiles outside \([0,1]\). It constructs a globally unique draw identifier from the tuple \((\texttt{model}, \texttt{run\_id}, \texttt{repeat\_index})\); \(\texttt{repeat\_index}\) alone is not unique enough.

The cleaning report found 13 invalid rows and 3587 usable rows. The SOTA subset contains 1798 usable rows, two fewer than the nominal 1800. The two missing SOTA rows occur in \texttt{T1584.005 - Resource Development: Build/Compromise Botnet} and \texttt{T1590 - Reconnaissance: Gather Victim Network Information}. All SOTA MITRE-step inputs retain rows from all five expected LLM forecaster models. The capability mapping from task names to SOTA or saturated condition was audited by MITRE-step label.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Fitting elicitation distributions}

Each valid SOTA elicitation row gives three quartiles \((q_{25},q_{50},q_{75})\) for one MITRE-step probability. The implementation fits one Beta distribution on \([0,1]\) to each row by quantile matching:
\[
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
=
\arg\min_{\alpha,\beta>0}
\sum_{\tau \in \{0.25,0.50,0.75\}}
\left[
Q_{\mathrm{Beta}}(\tau;\alpha,\beta)-q_{\tau}
\right]^2.
\]
Optimisation is performed in log-parameter space so that \(\alpha,\beta>0\). Exact endpoint quartiles would be clipped for fitting, but no SOTA rows required endpoint clipping in the generated fits.

Across 1798 SOTA rows, 1790 fits were flagged \(\texttt{ok}\), 8 were flagged \(\texttt{warn}\), and 0 failed. The median RMSE was approximately 0.00222, the 95th percentile RMSE was approximately 0.00688, and the maximum RMSE was approximately 0.02536. This is adequate for a proof-of-concept, but it remains a parametric approximation to elicited quartiles.

\subsection{OC3 DoS \texorpdfstring{$P(\mathrm{success})$}{P(success)} forward model}

The OC3 DoS \(\psuccess\) submodel has nine elicited leaf inputs. The short variables and model labels are:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
p_{\mathrm{active}} & \text{T1595 - Reconnaissance: Active Scanning},\\
p_{\mathrm{gather}} & \text{T1590 - Reconnaissance: Gather Victim Network Information},\\
p_{\mathrm{acquire}} & \text{T1583.005 - Resource Development: Acquire Botnet},\\
p_{\mathrm{build}} & \text{T1584.005 - Resource Development: Build/Compromise Botnet},\\
p_{\mathrm{masq}} & \text{T1036 - Defense Evasion: Masquerading},\\
p_{\mathrm{port}} & \text{T1571 - Defense Evasion: Non-Standard Port},\\
p_{\mathrm{c2}} & \text{TA0011 - Command-and-Control},\\
p_{\mathrm{direct}} & \text{T1498.001 - Impact: Direct Network Flood},\\
p_{\mathrm{refl}} & \text{T1498.002 - Impact: Reflection/Amplification Attack}.
\end{array}
\]

The tactic-level gates are:
\begin{align}
p_{\mathrm{rec}} &= p_{\mathrm{active}} + p_{\mathrm{gather}} - p_{\mathrm{active}}p_{\mathrm{gather}},\\
p_{\mathrm{res}} &= p_{\mathrm{build}} + p_{\mathrm{acquire}} - p_{\mathrm{build}}p_{\mathrm{acquire}},\\
p_{\mathrm{def}} &= p_{\mathrm{port}}p_{\mathrm{masq}},\\
p_{\mathrm{imp}} &= p_{\mathrm{direct}} + p_{\mathrm{refl}} - p_{\mathrm{direct}}p_{\mathrm{refl}}.
\end{align}
Command and Control is elicited directly as \(p_{\mathrm{c2}}\). The final success probability is
\[
\psuccess
=
p_{\mathrm{rec}}p_{\mathrm{res}}p_{\mathrm{def}}p_{\mathrm{c2}}p_{\mathrm{imp}}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\psuccess =
\left(p_{\mathrm{active}} + p_{\mathrm{gather}} - p_{\mathrm{active}}p_{\mathrm{gather}}\right)
\left(p_{\mathrm{build}} + p_{\mathrm{acquire}} - p_{\mathrm{build}}p_{\mathrm{acquire}}\right)
\left(p_{\mathrm{port}}p_{\mathrm{masq}}\right)
p_{\mathrm{c2}}
\left(p_{\mathrm{direct}} + p_{\mathrm{refl}} - p_{\mathrm{direct}}p_{\mathrm{refl}}\right).
\]

Forward-model smoke tests produced a full-reference Monte Carlo mean of approximately 0.2244 and median of approximately 0.2197. Deterministic plug-in checks were close: the mean fitted-Beta mean plug-in produced \(\psuccess \approx 0.2247\), and the median elicited-median plug-in produced \(\psuccess \approx 0.2203\).

\subsection{Exchangeable nodewise mixtures}

For MITRE-step input \(j\), let \(S_j\) be the set of currently revealed valid SOTA draws. The current node mixture is
\[
M_j(S_j)=\frac{1}{|S_j|}\sum_{d\in S_j}D_{j,d},
\]
where \(D_{j,d}\) is the fitted Beta distribution for draw \(d\) at node \(j\). To sample from \(M_j\), the implementation chooses a revealed fitted row uniformly and then samples from its Beta distribution. Node samples are drawn independently across the nine MITRE-step inputs before propagation through the forward model.

This exchangeable nodewise mixture is a modelling approximation. It captures the marginal spread of elicited views at each node, but it does not preserve cross-node coherence from a single LLM forecaster model or repeat.

\subsection{Full-data reference distribution}

The full-data reference uses all valid SOTA rows for each node:
\[
M_j^{\mathrm{full}}
=
\frac{1}{|S_j^{\mathrm{full}}|}
\sum_{d\in S_j^{\mathrm{full}}}D_{j,d}.
\]
The reference output distribution is
\[
T(M^{\mathrm{full}})=\law\left(P_{M^{\mathrm{full}}}(\mathrm{success})\right).
\]
It is an internal retrospective reference, not truth.

\subsection{Budgeted reveal protocol}

All policies begin with the same initial seed allocation for each reveal seed: five rows per MITRE-step input, one from each LLM forecaster model, for a total initial budget of 45 SOTA elicitation rows. Additional allocation decisions reveal one fitted row at a selected MITRE-step input. Within a selected input, the implementation uses seed-controlled random selection from the remaining unrevealed rows. Policy comparisons are paired by reveal seed and use a common full-reference sample. This development implementation therefore gives reproducible paired policy comparisons, while keeping the budget unit as one LLM elicitation draw.

The v8 plan describes a more explicitly materialised hidden reveal-order protocol, with model-aware cycling after the initial seed allocation. The present report uses the generated development outputs from the implemented seeded reveal machinery; this should be verified before a final locked experiment.

\subsection{LOO output fragility}

Given revealed draw sets \(S_1,\ldots,S_9\), let \(M\) be the current exchangeable nodewise mixture model. For a draw \(d\in S_j\), define
\[
M_j^{(-d)}
=
\frac{1}{|S_j|-1}
\sum_{d'\in S_j,\,d'\neq d}D_{j,d'}.
\]
Let \(M^{(j,-d)}\) be the full model equal to \(M\) except that node \(j\)'s mixture is replaced by \(M_j^{(-d)}\). The LOO fragility score is
\[
F_j^{\mathrm{LOO}}
=
\frac{1}{|S_j|}
\sum_{d\in S_j}
\wtsq\left(T(M^{(j,-d)}),T(M)\right).
\]
This score measures the output movement caused by removing currently observed draws from a node. It is a finite-sample instability diagnostic, not expected information gain.

For runtime reasons, the development policy runs used approximate LOO-term subsampling with \(\texttt{max\_loo\_terms\_per\_step}=20\). A separate approximation audit compared exact LOO with capped subsampling on step-balanced revealed states. In that reduced audit, cap 20 preserved the exact top fragile node in all audited comparisons, had mean top-3 Jaccard approximately 0.833, and had mean Spearman rank correlation approximately 0.833. Cap 10 was noisier and was marked questionable by the audit heuristic. This supports cap 20 as a development approximation, but it is not a proof that the approximation is exact.

\subsection{Allocation policies}

The implemented v8 policy families are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Step-balanced uniform allocation.} Allocate to eligible MITRE-step inputs with the smallest current revealed count. This is the main baseline.
    \item \textbf{Greedy LOO-fragility allocation.} Allocate to the eligible input with the largest current \(F_j^{\mathrm{LOO}}\).
    \item \textbf{Epsilon-greedy LOO-fragility allocation.} With probability \(0.8\), exploit by choosing the eligible input with largest fragility. With probability \(0.2\), explore by choosing among currently under-sampled eligible inputs.
    \item \textbf{Exploration-bonus LOO-fragility allocation.} Choose the eligible input maximizing
    \[
    A_j = F_j^{\mathrm{LOO}} + \lambda b(n_j),
    \quad
    b(n_j)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_j}},
    \quad
    \lambda = c \cdot \mathrm{median}\{F_j^{\mathrm{LOO}}: F_j^{\mathrm{LOO}}>0\},
    \]
    where \(n_j=|S_j|\). Sensitivity runs evaluated \(c\in\{0.25,0.5,1.0\}\).
\end{enumerate}

Fragility scores are recomputed in batches in the development runs. This keeps the experiment feasible but means that each batch uses the most recently computed score table.

\subsection{Evaluation metric}

The main error metric is squared Wasserstein-2 distance to the full-data reference:
\[
\mathrm{Err}(M)=\wtsq\left(T(M),T(M^{\mathrm{full}})\right).
\]
For one-dimensional empirical distributions with quantile functions \(Q_A\) and \(Q_B\),
\[
\wtsq(A,B)=\int_0^1 \left(Q_A(u)-Q_B(u)\right)^2\,du.
\]
Thus the reported error is a mean squared quantile discrepancy between the current budgeted output distribution and the full-data reference.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Beta-fitting and forward-model sanity checks}

The data cleaning, Beta fitting, and forward-model smoke tests did not reveal blocking issues. The SOTA cleaning output retained 1798 usable rows. Beta quantile matching produced 1790 \(\texttt{ok}\) fits, 8 \(\texttt{warn}\) fits, and no failed fits. The warned fits were retained in the fitted dataset and used by the mixture model.

The full-data exchangeable reference Monte Carlo sample had mean \(\psuccess\) approximately 0.2244 and median approximately 0.2197. The deterministic plug-in checks based on fitted Beta means and elicited medians were close to this range. These checks support the implementation of the forward-model mapping and gate structure.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation}

The results suggest that output fragility contains some useful signal about where additional elicitation draws can reduce recovery error. Greedy LOO-fragility and exploration-bonus \(c=1.0\) both had lower average AUC than the step-balanced uniform baseline in the generated development outputs.

At the same time, the evidence is mixed. No fragility-guided policy beat uniform on a majority of paired seed-budget comparisons. The policies with better AUC also concentrated elicitation effort strongly. This suggests that LOO output fragility can identify influential or unstable inputs, but using it too directly risks over-concentration. Exploration-aware variants partially mitigate this problem, with the \(c=1.0\) exploration-bonus setting looking best among the tested values in the current sensitivity run.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore not that fragility guidance is solved or optimal. A narrower conclusion is that the heuristic appears to have signal, but final conclusions require a locked final configuration, more reveal seeds if runtime permits, and careful reporting of concentration as well as recovery error.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations are central to interpreting these results:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The budget unit is an LLM elicitation draw, not an independent human expert.
    \item The experiment is retrospective. All candidate draws already exist and are hidden from the policy until revealed.
    \item The full-data reference is not ground truth. It is constructed from finite repeated LLM draws.
    \item The analysis is restricted to OC3 Denial of Service, the SOTA condition, and the \(\psuccess\) submodel.
    \item The report does not model full total-risk uncertainty.
    \item The primary output is \(\psuccess\), not \(\log \psuccess\).
    \item Beta distributions are parametric approximations to elicited quartiles.
    \item LOO fragility is a finite-sample instability heuristic, not expected information gain.
    \item Development policy runs used approximate LOO-term subsampling with \(\texttt{max\_loo\_terms\_per\_step}=20\).
    \item Some comparisons in the report-ready tables combine outputs from different source runs. This is explicitly labelled and should not be hidden.
    \item The number of reveal seeds is modest.
    \item Runtime constraints shaped the experiment design, including batching of fragility recomputation and the reduced all-policy development run.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future work}

Natural next steps include exact LOO final runs on a smaller budget schedule, stronger approximation audits, alternative acquisition rules, model-balanced and model-coherent reference distributions, a log-output analysis, extension to full total risk once the remaining factors are elicited, application to human expert elicitation data, bootstrap fragility, and replication on other SaferAI risk models.

\section{Conclusion}

This draft reports a retrospective budget-recovery experiment for the OC3 DoS \(\psuccess\) submodel using repeated LLM elicitation draws. The results provide preliminary evidence that output-fragility-guided allocation can reduce average recovery error in some settings, but also that greedy fragility guidance can severely over-concentrate elicitation effort. Exploration bonuses reduce concentration relative to lower-exploration variants, and \(c=1.0\) looked best among the tested exploration-bonus settings in the development sensitivity run. These findings are best viewed as evidence that the heuristic warrants further investigation, not as a final claim of optimal allocation.

\section{Implementation and reproducibility notes}

The implementation is an ordinary Python package and script workflow rather than a notebook workflow. The generated report artifacts used here are located in \(\texttt{outputs/report\_artifacts/}\), and the figures are copied from \(\texttt{figures/}\). The report-ready tables combine \(\texttt{v8\_all\_policies\_dev}\) and \(\texttt{exploration\_bonus\_sensitivity\_dev}\) outputs.
